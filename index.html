      What this is

      The flat-earth model puts the Sun, Moon and stars on a dome above a flat map. This site takes the real sky, computed with standard astronomy for a round Earth, and places every object on that dome. Then it asks what light would have to do so that people still see the sky they actually see.

      Every part of the dome model can be adjusted: the dome’s height, width and shape, and whether the Sun and Moon ride on the dome or circle below it at a height and size you choose. None of these settings brings the Sun below the horizon; try it.

      The answer is drawn as curved light rays. On a globe the same rays are straight; switch to Globe to compare.

      How to use it

      • Dome and Globe: drag to turn the camera, pinch or scroll to zoom.
      • Sky view: what a person at the red-and-blue marker sees. Swipe or scroll sideways to turn east or west.
      • The bar at the bottom changes the time. Press play for a time-lapse.

      Accuracy

      Terrain is an approximate model of the major mountain ranges, plateaus and ice sheets, placed and sized from standard atlases, with heights exaggerated (20× by default) only so their shape shows on a map 40,000 km across. Antarctica alone is raised far higher: the ring around the rim shows the “ice wall” that flat-earth maps describe; its height is a setting, not a measurement. The real Antarctic coast is mostly ice cliffs tens of metres high, with an ice sheet up to 4 km thick behind them.

      Sun, Moon, planets and Earth's rotation come from standard low-precision formulas, accurate to a few arcminutes for 2025–2030. Stars: about 190 of the brightest, to about 0.1°. The sky view includes atmospheric refraction. The curved rays are drawn shapes that join the two ends; the dome model itself offers no law of physics for them.

      Privacy

      No accounts, no cookies, no ads, no analytics. The site makes no requests to other servers. Your last view is remembered in this browser only. “Use my location” stays on your device unless you share a link, which includes the observer’s position.
